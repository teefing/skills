## Distilled Model

### 1. Growth Is Not Only Promotion

Treat career growth as full-stack personal evolution:

- Skill depth: technical or professional craft.
- Problem ownership: ability to define, break down, and drive ambiguous work.
- Business cognition: understanding why work matters.
- Collaboration: aligning people and expectations.
- Judgment: making better tradeoffs under incomplete information.
- Identity: becoming more stable, self-directed, and clear about one's standards.

### 2. Stage-Based Growth

Use stage language cautiously; calibrate by capability rather than years.

Early stage:

- Build reliability.
- Ask clear questions.
- Communicate progress early.
- Learn team norms and business context.
- Do not hide uncertainty.

Middle stage:

- Own problems end to end.
- Frame tradeoffs, not just execute assigned tasks.
- Improve cross-functional communication.
- Make methods reusable.
- Build a visible record of impact.

Later stage:

- Improve judgment and leverage.
- Shape direction, not only output.
- Mentor others through systems and examples.
- Operate across ambiguity and organizational boundaries.
- Make peace with becoming a distinct kind of contributor, not a copy of someone else.

### 3. Potential Signals

Look for and cultivate these behaviors:

- High standards with execution.
- Strong goal sense and self-drive.
- Ability to recover from setbacks.
- Willingness to listen and adjust.
- Fit between personal interest and work.
- Curiosity about business, users, and systems.
- Habit of turning feedback into experiments.

### 4. Business Growth And Personal Growth

When business is fast-growing, opportunities may be abundant. When business slows or plateaus, growth comes from:

- Raising cognition.
- Solving more essential problems.
- Improving methods.
- Building reusable tools or playbooks.
- Finding value in constraints.
- Choosing work by impact and learning, not only by visible scope.

### 5. Soft Skills Are Work Skills

Communication, expectation management, feedback, planning, and influence are not separate from technical work. They determine whether good technical work becomes trusted, adopted, and repeated.

Practical behaviors:

- State assumptions.
- Sync early when risk changes.
- Write down decisions.
- Ask for examples when feedback is vague.
- Make progress visible.
- Clarify ownership and next actions.

## Anti-Patterns

- Counting busyness as growth.
- Waiting for perfect clarity before acting.
- Treating promotion as the only proof of development.
- Only doing assigned tasks without understanding why.
- Avoiding stakeholder communication until work is finished.
- Recording outputs but not decisions, tradeoffs, or impact.
- Taking feedback as identity judgment instead of behavior data.
- Equating large scope with high value.

## Coaching Questions

Use these questions when the user needs deeper reflection:

- What problem did this work actually solve?
- What became clearer because of your work?
- What did you own that others did not have to worry about?
- Which decision changed the outcome most?
- What tradeoff did you make, and why was it reasonable?
- Who trusted you more after this work, and what evidence shows that?
- What capability became more repeatable?
- What would you do 20 percent earlier next time?
- What should become a checklist, template, or habit?
